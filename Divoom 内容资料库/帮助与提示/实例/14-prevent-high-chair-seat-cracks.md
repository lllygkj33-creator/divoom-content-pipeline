# How to Prevent High Chair Seat Cracks From Trapping Food and Bacteria

The short answer is to choose a high chair with smooth, nonporous, easily cleanable surfaces, clean food-contact areas after each use, and take any crack that traps visible food seriously. [If a seat crack](https://www.cdc.gov/hygiene/about/how-to-clean-and-disinfect-early-care-and-education-settings.html) can’t be cleaned out well, it’s a hygiene problem, not just a cosmetic one, and the chair should be repaired or replaced.

## Why cracks and seams matter

Food and moisture sitting in cracks make it harder to remove germs fully. The CDC says to [clean](https://www.cdc.gov/hygiene/cleaning-disinfecting/index.html) first, because dirt and impurities reduce how well sanitizers and disinfectants work.

For high chairs, the key concern is any break, open seam, crack, chip, or crevice that holds onto crumbs or moisture after cleaning. FDA Food Code guidance for cleanable food-contact surfaces treats those spaces as a sanitation risk when they can’t be kept smooth and easily cleanable.

## What to look for when choosing a high chair

Pick a chair that has fewer seams, fewer joints, and surfaces that wipe down easily. Smooth, nonabsorbent materials are easier to keep clean than designs with lots of hidden edges and deep grooves.

Look for removable parts that you can actually take off and clean separately, especially trays, seat pads, and any fabric pieces. The easier it is to access the area under the seat and around the joints, the less likely crumbs are to stay tucked away. ### Good signs in a seat design

- Smooth seat surface
- Minimal cracks and seams
- Removable tray
- Removable cushion or pad
- Easy access under the seat
- No trapped pockets where food or liquid can sit

If a chair has visible damage, worn padding, or a crack that keeps catching food, that’s a warning sign that the design is no longer staying hygienic in real life.

## Daily cleaning that keeps food from settling into cracks

Clean the tray and seat after each use so crumbs do not dry inside the seams. CDC guidance for high-chair trays says they should be cleaned with hot, soapy water and sanitized before and after each use because they contact children’s mouths.

[For hand washing](https://www.cdc.gov/hygiene/about/clean-sanitize-store-infant-feeding-items.html), rinse the parts under running water, wash them in a separate basin with hot water and soap, and use a dedicated brush to work into crevices. A small brush, like a toothbrush, helps loosen crumbs from buckle slots, hinges, and seam edges.

![Illustrated step-by-step guide for daily high chair cleaning routine](https://cdn.shopify.com/s/files/1/0559/2321/2486/files/Illustrated_step_by_step_guide_for_daily_high_chai.png?v=1788331240)

After washing, rinse away soap residue and let everything air-dry completely before putting the chair back together. Moisture left in cracks can encourage mold and bacterial growth.

### Simple daily routine

1. Wipe off visible food right after the meal.
2. Wash the tray, seat, and frame with hot, soapy water.
3. Scrub seams, hinges, and corners with a small brush.
4. Rinse thoroughly.
5. Air-dry completely before reassembling.

## Weekly and deep-clean steps for hidden buildup

Once a week, or more often if your baby is a messy eater, do a more complete clean. Remove the seat pad if it comes off, clean underneath it, and check strap areas, joints, and the underside of the seat for crumbs.

If the chair is [dishwasher](https://www.cdc.gov/hygiene/faq/index.html)-safe, disassemble the parts, rinse them, place small pieces in a closed-top basket, and use hot water with a heated drying cycle or sanitizing setting. That helps when the parts are designed for it, but you should still follow the manufacturer’s instructions first.

![Diagram showing deep cleaning focus areas on disassembled high chair parts](https://cdn.shopify.com/s/files/1/0559/2321/2486/files/Diagram_showing_deep_cleaning_focus_areas_on_disas.png?v=1788331255)

If you sanitize after cleaning, CDC guidance says not to rinse the items afterward; instead, let them air-dry completely on a clean, unused dish towel or paper towel.

### When a deep clean helps most

- After messy meals
- When crumbs keep reappearing in the same seams
- When fabric covers can be removed
- When the tray, cushion, or straps have built-up residue
- When you notice odor, discoloration, or sticky spots

## When cleaning is not enough

If a crack keeps trapping food after washing, that chair is no longer doing its job as an easily cleanable surface. FDA guidance says equipment with inaccessible cracks or surfaces that can’t be adequately cleaned should be repaired or replaced, and if the crack can’t be effectively cleaned, it should be taken out of service until fixed.

Visible damage matters most when it creates a place where food and moisture stay behind. A temporary repair only makes sense if it results in a smooth, nonabsorbent, easily cleanable surface that can hold up to repeated cleaning.

![Close-up of cracked high chair seat with trapped food debris](https://cdn.shopify.com/s/files/1/0559/2321/2486/files/Close_up_of_cracked_high_chair_seat_with_trapped_f.png?v=1788331270)

### Stop using the chair and fix or replace it if:

- Food stays stuck after normal cleaning
- The crack is widening or breaking apart
- Padding is worn and trapping debris
- A seam or joint cannot be cleaned out
- The surface is no longer smooth or easy to wipe clean

## The safest cleaning pattern to remember

Think in this order: clean first, sanitize second if needed, dry completely, and then store it clean. CDC guidance also notes that disinfecting is generally reserved for situations where someone is sick or there is another higher-risk reason, and surfaces must be cleaned before any disinfectant is used.

For most high chairs, the practical goal is not just to make the chair look clean. It’s to keep food from sitting in cracks long enough to become a germ problem in the first place.

A good rule is this: if you can’t get a seam clean with a normal wash, brush, rinse, and full dry, the chair is telling you it has become too hard to maintain safely. Take that seriously and move to repair or replacement.
